      title: Research
      text: |
        The LENS Lab is interested in semiconductor optoelectronic devices —
        how they are made, how they work, and how they fail. We approach
        these questions through a combination of materials synthesis,
        thin-film processing, device fabrication, and multimodal
        characterisation, with the goal of building a quantitative
        understanding of loss mechanisms across complex multilayer stacks.

        Our research interests span three directions, which we see as
        mutually reinforcing: building the fabrication and characterisation
        infrastructure to study materials systematically; developing
        high-performance multilayer optoelectronic devices; and extending
        these platforms toward solar fuels and emerging device applications.

  - block: markdown
    content:
      title: Multilayer Optoelectronic Devices
      text: |
        ![Multilayer optoelectronic devices](research-devices.jpg)

        Perovskite semiconductors are attractive for multilayer device
        architectures because of their broad bandgap tunability, high
        defect tolerance, and compatibility with solution and vapour
        processing. In photovoltaics, monolithic multijunction stacks
        can capture more of the solar spectrum than any single absorber,
        with efficiency potentials that exceed fundamental single-junction
        limits.

        We are interested in understanding and reducing the energetic and
        optical losses that limit performance in these architectures —
        including non-radiative recombination at interfaces, compositional
        disorder in wide-bandgap absorbers, and optical losses in
        multilayer stacks. The same materials and device design principles
        extend to vertically stacked photodetectors and light-emitting
        devices, where multilayer architectures can enable wavelength
        selectivity and spectral control not achievable in simpler devices.

        Research directions include multijunction photovoltaics (all-perovskite
        and perovskite-on-silicon), perovskite LEDs, and spectrally
        selective photodetectors.

  - block: markdown
    content:
      title: Fabrication Platform and Multimodal Characterisation
      text: |
        ![Fabrication and characterisation platform](research-platforms.jpg)

        Advancing multilayer device performance requires not only new
        materials but also the infrastructure to study them systematically.
        We are developing integrated thin-film fabrication and characterisation
        workflows — combining solution processing, vacuum deposition, and
        a range of structural, optical, and electronic measurement
        techniques — to establish rigorous, reproducible methods for
        understanding intrinsic material and interface properties.

        A particular interest is in combining high-throughput fabrication
        with multimodal in-situ and operando characterisation, allowing
        composition, processing, and interface variables to be explored
        across a wide parameter space. These platforms are intended to
        support both fundamental understanding and the translation of
        laboratory findings into scalable processes.

        Relevant capabilities include automated solution processing,
        multi-source vacuum deposition (thermal evaporation, sputtering,
        ALD, PLD), and characterisation under stress, illumination,
        and electrical bias.

  - block: markdown
    content:
      title: Solar Fuels, High-Energy Detection, and Emerging Platforms
      text: |
        ![Solar fuels and emerging device platforms](research-solar-fuels.jpg)

        High-voltage perovskite multijunction devices can generate
        sufficient photovoltage to drive electrochemical reactions
        directly — producing hydrogen by water splitting, or reducing
        carbon dioxide into chemical fuels, using sunlight as the
        sole energy input. We are interested in the performance,
        stability, and integration of tandem photovoltaic devices
        coupled to electrochemical systems, including the materials
        and interface challenges that arise at the boundary between
        photovoltaics and electrochemistry.

        Beyond solar fuels, we have a broader interest in emerging
        device platforms where the multilayer semiconductor design
        principles developed for photovoltaics can open new possibilities.
        These include high-energy radiation detection using perovskite
        absorbers, novel flexible and multi-functional optoelectronic
        devices, and new semiconductor materials whose properties remain
        to be fully understood and exploited.

        These are longer-term directions that we expect to develop
        alongside our core photovoltaics programme.
